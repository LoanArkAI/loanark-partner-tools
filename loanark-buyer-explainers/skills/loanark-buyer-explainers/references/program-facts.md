# Program facts for buyer and seller explainers

These are the only financing facts the explainers may state. They are written the way they may appear in agent content: how a program works, who it is generally for, what comes up along the way. There are no rates, payments, dollar amounts, down-payment percentages, terms, or qualification promises here on purpose; those are Reg Z trigger terms or Loan Ark quotes, and both belong to the loan officer. Numbers that only describe a program's structure (a 2-1 buydown's two years, the three-business-day disclosure rule) are fine.

Where a fact says "generally" or "often", keep the hedge. Programs change; the LO has the current rules. Loan Ark lends in California, Colorado, and Texas.

---

## First-time buyers: the process, start to finish

1. **Talk to a loan officer before you shop.** A conversation and a document review turn "I think we can" into a pre-approval you can write offers with.
2. **Pre-approval.** The LO reviews income, assets, and credit and tells you what you can shop for. The letter is what a listing agent reads next to your offer.
3. **Shop with your agent.** Tours, questions, offers. The agent handles the contract; the LO stands by to update the letter for a specific home.
4. **Offer accepted.** The contract goes to the LO the same day. The clock starts on disclosures, appraisal, and contingencies.
5. **Disclosures.** Within three business days of a full application, the lender sends the Loan Estimate, a standard form that lays out the loan. Sign the initial package promptly; the appraisal usually cannot be ordered until you have received it and said you want to proceed.
6. **Appraisal and inspection.** The lender orders the appraisal (an independent opinion of value). The buyer arranges the inspection (the condition of the home). They are different things, done by different people.
7. **Underwriting.** An underwriter reviews the whole file and issues a conditional approval with a short list of items still needed. Then "clear to close."
8. **Closing Disclosure.** At least three business days before signing, the final numbers arrive on a standard form. Read it; ask the LO about anything that looks different from the Loan Estimate.
9. **Signing, funding, keys.** Sign (usually with a notary or at the title company, depending on the state), the lender funds, the deed records, the agent hands over keys.

Things that quietly derail a first purchase: changing jobs mid-process, opening new credit or financing furniture before closing, moving money between accounts without a paper trail, and large cash deposits. Tell the LO before doing any of them.

"First-time buyer" for program purposes often means someone who has not owned a home in the last three years, so a previous owner can qualify again. Some programs use their own definition; the LO checks.

## What "pre-approved" means

- **Pre-qualification** is an estimate from a conversation. Nothing is verified.
- **Pre-approval** means the LO has reviewed actual documents (pay stubs, W-2s or tax returns, bank statements, ID) and pulled credit, and a lender has said what it is prepared to lend, subject to the property and final underwriting.
- A pre-approval letter is typically good for a couple of months; the LO refreshes it if the search runs long, and can tailor it to a specific offer so the letter does not overshoot the price.
- What the letter does for the buyer: listing agents and sellers weigh it when comparing offers. A verified pre-approval from a lender who answers the phone is a real advantage in a multiple-offer situation.
- What it does not do: it is not a loan commitment. The home has to appraise, the file has to underwrite, and the buyer's situation has to stay the same.

## VA loans

- For eligible veterans, active-duty service members, and some surviving spouses. Eligibility is proven with a Certificate of Eligibility, which the LO can usually pull.
- May allow the buyer to purchase with no down payment, and has no monthly mortgage insurance.
- There is a one-time VA funding fee that can be financed into the loan; some borrowers are exempt. The LO checks the exemption.
- The property gets a VA appraisal with minimum property requirements (safe, sound, sanitary), so certain repairs can be required before closing.
- Sellers are allowed to pay certain buyer costs; the contract can be written to use that.
- VA loans are assumable by a qualified buyer later, which can be a selling point when the veteran moves.
- Common myths, all false: "VA loans take forever," "sellers won't accept them," "you can only use it once."

## Jumbo loans

- A jumbo loan is any mortgage larger than the conforming limit that the Federal Housing Finance Agency sets each year. The limit is higher in designated high-cost counties, which includes much of coastal California.
- Because Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac do not buy jumbo loans, each lender sets its own rules. Expect more documentation, a closer look at reserves (money left after closing), and sometimes a second appraisal.
- Down payment and credit expectations vary by lender and by loan size; the LO knows Loan Ark's current jumbo guidelines and whether the buyer's numbers fit.
- Some buyers near the limit choose a conforming first mortgage plus a second loan instead of one jumbo; the LO can compare.

## Financing an ADU

- An accessory dwelling unit (casita, garage conversion, detached cottage, in-law unit) can be financed several ways: a cash-out refinance of the main home, a home equity line or loan, a renovation loan that lends against the value after the work (FHA 203(k), Fannie Mae HomeStyle, Freddie Mac CHOICERenovation are the common ones), or a construction loan.
- On a purchase, some programs allow a portion of the ADU's expected rental income to help the buyer qualify. Whether it counts, and how much, depends on the program and the paperwork.
- California state law has made ADU permitting easier in recent years, but the city still issues the permit and sets the rules. The lender will want permits and a licensed contractor's bid.
- Appraisers value ADUs differently from market to market. Ask before assuming the unit adds a specific amount of value.
- ADU rules vary by state and city; check local zoning before planning the unit.

## The 2-1 buydown

- A temporary buydown usually paid for by the seller or builder, sometimes the lender; the buyer rarely funds it. The money goes into an escrow account at closing.
- The buyer's effective payment is based on a rate two percentage points below the note rate in year one and one point below in year two; from year three on, the full note rate applies for the rest of the loan.
- The buyer qualifies at the full note rate, not the reduced one, so the buydown does not stretch what they can afford.
- If the buyer refinances or sells early, the unused buydown money is generally credited back on the loan.
- It is a negotiating tool: a seller offering a buydown instead of a price cut can make a home more affordable in the early years without lowering the price. Whether it beats a price cut for a specific buyer is math the LO runs.
- A permanent buydown (paying points) is a different thing; do not mix them up in the explainer.

## Assumable loans

- FHA, VA, and USDA loans are generally assumable: a qualified buyer can take over the seller's existing loan, including its rate and remaining balance, with the servicer's approval. Most conventional loans are not assumable.
- The buyer still has to qualify with the loan's servicer, and the process runs on the servicer's timeline, often longer than a new loan.
- The gap between the loan balance and the purchase price has to be covered by the buyer's cash or a second loan, which is the part that makes many assumptions hard in practice.
- On a VA assumption, the seller's VA entitlement can stay tied up unless the buyer is also an eligible veteran who substitutes their own; the seller should understand this before agreeing.
- Listing agents can ask the seller for their current loan type and servicer; if it is FHA or VA, the assumption may be worth mentioning in marketing as a possibility, never as a promise.

## Seller credits vs. a price cut

- A **seller credit** (concession) is money the seller agrees to pay toward the buyer's closing costs, prepaid items, or a buydown. A **price cut** lowers the purchase price and, with it, the loan amount.
- Each loan program caps how much a seller can contribute, and the cap depends on the loan type and down payment. The LO knows the cap for this buyer before the offer is written.
- The shape of the tradeoff: a credit usually helps the buyer's cash needed at closing more than an equal price cut helps their monthly payment, because a price cut is spread over the life of the loan. Which one a buyer wants depends on whether their problem is cash today or payment every month.
- A credit cannot exceed the buyer's actual costs; anything above that is lost, so the number should come from the LO's estimate, not a round figure.
- The home still has to appraise at the contract price. A credit does not change the price the appraiser is checking.
- Tax and legal effects of either approach are for the buyer's and seller's own advisors.

## You're in contract: what happens now

- **Day one:** the agent sends the contract to the LO. Send any updated documents the LO asks for that day.
- **Disclosures:** sign the initial package as soon as it arrives; the appraisal usually cannot be ordered until it is signed.
- **Inspection:** scheduled by the buyer and agent, early in the contingency period.
- **Appraisal:** ordered by the lender through an independent process; neither the agent nor the LO can choose the appraiser or talk to them about value. If the appraisal comes in below the contract price, there are options, and the LO and agent walk the buyer through them.
- **Insurance:** the buyer gets a homeowners insurance quote early. In parts of California, Colorado, and Texas (fire, hail, wind, flood zones) this takes longer than people expect, and the lender needs proof of insurance before closing.
- **HOA:** if there is one, the HOA documents are ordered and reviewed; some associations are slow.
- **Underwriting:** conditional approval, then a short list of conditions, then clear to close.
- **Closing Disclosure:** at least three business days before signing.
- **Signing and funding:** the buyer signs, the lender funds, and the sale records. Customs differ by state: California closes through escrow, Colorado and Texas typically close at a title company. The agent and LO walk the buyer through their state's version.
- **Contingencies:** the contract sets deadlines for inspection, appraisal, and loan contingencies (Texas calls the inspection window the option period and handles financing through its own addendum). The agent tracks them; the LO's job is to keep the loan ahead of them.

What the buyer should do during this stretch: answer the LO the same day, keep their finances exactly as they were at pre-approval, and ask before making any change.

## Seller's guide: from listing to close

1. **Preparation:** repairs, staging, photos, and the pricing conversation with the agent.
2. **Disclosures:** each state has required seller disclosure forms (California's Transfer Disclosure Statement and Natural Hazard Disclosure, Colorado's Seller's Property Disclosure, Texas's Seller's Disclosure Notice are the usual ones). Fill them out completely and truthfully; the agent explains each.
3. **Listing and showings.**
4. **Offers:** price is one line; the rest matters too. The agent reads each buyer's financing: loan type, down payment, pre-approval quality, and whether the buyer's lender answers the phone. The listing agent is allowed to call the buyer's LO to verify the pre-approval.
5. **Under contract:** the buyer's inspection and appraisal happen, contingencies are removed on schedule, and repair requests are negotiated.
6. **Seller credits and buydowns:** tools for keeping a deal together or for marketing a home; see the seller-credit and 2-1 buydown facts above.
7. **Closing:** the seller often signs first or separately, the sale funds and records, and proceeds are wired.
8. **If the seller is also buying:** talk to the LO early about the order of operations, bridge options, and what "contingent on the sale of my home" does to an offer.

## Down payment help: where it comes from

- **Savings** and money from selling something (with a paper trail).
- **Gifts** from family, documented with a gift letter and a record of the transfer. Most programs allow gifts; the rules on who can give differ by program.
- **Down payment assistance programs:** state housing finance agencies (CalHFA in California, CHFA in Colorado, TSAHC and TDHCA in Texas), plus city and county programs and employer-assisted programs. Each has its own eligibility, income limits, and availability, and funds come and go. Describe them as "may be available for eligible buyers"; the LO confirms which ones this buyer can actually use.
- **Retirement accounts:** some plans allow loans or withdrawals for a first home; the tax consequences belong to a tax advisor.
- **Seller credits** generally go toward closing costs, not the down payment; the LO explains the line between the two.
- **Low-down-payment programs:** FHA and conventional programs exist for buyers with limited savings, and VA may require no down payment for eligible borrowers. Never state a percentage in agent content.

## Conventional and FHA, for context

- **Conventional (conforming)** loans follow Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac rules. When the down payment is smaller, private mortgage insurance is usually added to the payment and can be removed later once enough equity builds.
- **FHA** loans are insured by the Federal Housing Administration, are often used by first-time buyers and buyers rebuilding credit, allow a low down payment, and carry an upfront and an annual mortgage insurance premium. FHA has its own appraisal standards and county loan limits, and FHA loans are assumable.
- Which is better for a given buyer depends on credit, down payment, and how long they plan to stay. That comparison is the LO's.
